Macerating Toilet vs. Traditional Toilet: Which Should You Install in Your Basement in 2026? Simple Project

✓Last updated: September 03, 2026
⏱11 min read
By Best Home Decor Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

Adding a bathroom below grade is one of the highest-return renovations you can do — and also one of the easiest to get wrong. The core decision isn't tile or vanity style. It's whether you install a macerating (upflush) toilet that pumps waste up to your existing drain line, or a traditional gravity toilet that requires breaking the concrete slab and installing a sewage ejector pit. That single choice can swing your project cost by $3,000 or more.

To make this a real comparison rather than a sales pitch, we looked at four specific paths homeowners are actually choosing in 2026: Simple Project upflush and smart toilet systems, the long-established Saniflo SaniAccess and Sanicompact line, the American-made Liberty Pumps Ascent II, and the old-school route — a standard Kohler or American Standard gravity toilet plumbed into a Zoeller or Liberty sewage ejector basin.

None of these wins across the board. The right answer depends on whether your basement is finished, how far you are from the stack, whether you're renting the space out, and how much you care about long-term serviceability versus upfront cost. Here's the honest breakdown.

Simple Project: Best value for a complete, modern-looking upflush setup

Simple Project has carved out the mid-price slot in the macerating category, with complete upflush toilet systems typically landing in the $400–$700 range versus $900–$1,300 for comparable Saniflo or Liberty kits. What you get for that is a one-piece or skirted bowl that looks like a normal modern toilet rather than an obvious appliance, a macerator with roughly 15–16 ft of vertical lift and up to about 150 ft horizontal run, and side inlets so you can tie in a basement sink or shower. Their smart toilet crossover models — bidet wash, heated seat, auto flush — are the differentiator here; almost nobody else is putting genuine smart-toilet features on a macerating base at this price. The trade-offs are real, though: the dealer network is thin compared to Saniflo, so replacement blades, motors and seals generally come from the brand directly rather than a local plumbing supply shelf. If you want the most contemporary-looking basement bathroom for the least money and you're comfortable ordering parts online, this is the strongest value play in 2026.

Saniflo (SaniAccess 3 / Sanicompact 48): The category standard with the best parts availability

Saniflo essentially invented the residential macerating toilet and it shows in the ecosystem. The SaniAccess 3 runs around $1,000–$1,200 as a system and its headline feature is the access panels — you can open the macerator housing and service the blades and motor without pulling the entire unit off the wall, which is a genuinely meaningful advantage five years into ownership. The Sanicompact 48 is a self-contained 1-piece unit at around $1,000 that uses only 1 gallon per flush, ideal for a tight powder room under the stairs, though it can't accept a shower drain. Saniflo units are stocked at Ferguson, Home Depot and most plumbing wholesalers, so a plumber can source parts same-day. The downsides: the bowls look dated and boxy next to newer designs, the pumps are noticeably loud (roughly 55–60 dB during a 10–15 second cycle), and you're paying a real premium for the brand name and the distribution network.

Liberty Pumps Ascent II: The quietest and most reliable macerator, at a price

If budget is not the constraint, the Liberty Pumps Ascent II (roughly $1,200–$1,500 with the toilet) is the macerating unit most plumbers recommend when the customer says "just do it right." It's US-made in Michigan, uses a 1 HP motor, and Liberty's own testing plus field reports put it meaningfully quieter than Saniflo equivalents. The killer feature is the removable pump cartridge — the entire pumping assembly lifts out for service without disconnecting plumbing, which turns a two-hour job into a fifteen-minute one. It also handles 25 ft of lift, more than most competitors. Liberty backs it with a 3-year warranty. The catch is simply price: you're paying roughly double a Simple Project system for durability and serviceability that matter most in a rental, a daily-use family bathroom, or a home you plan to keep for a decade.

Traditional gravity toilet + sewage ejector pit: Still the best long-term answer if you're breaking concrete anyway

The conventional route means renting a concrete saw, cutting a trench, setting a sealed basin with a Zoeller M267 or Liberty Pumps LE51A ejector pump, and running a vent. A standard Kohler Cimarron or American Standard Champion 4 costs $200–$400; the ejector system adds $400–$900 in parts. But labor is where it lands: expect $2,500–$6,000 installed depending on slab thickness and distance to the main. In exchange you get a toilet with no motor in it, a 20+ year service life, no blade jams from wipes or hygiene products, silent flushing, and full resale value — appraisers and buyers treat a slab-plumbed bathroom as a real bathroom. Also worth noting: some local codes and some HOA or rental inspectors still look sideways at macerating units as the sole toilet in a legal bedroom-plus-bath basement suite. If your basement is already gutted to the studs, the math often favors doing it once, properly.

Head-to-head: cost, noise, install time

Rough 2026 numbers, DIY-installed where applicable: Simple Project upflush, $400–$700, 3–5 hours, no concrete work. Saniflo SaniAccess 3, $1,000–$1,200, 4–6 hours, best parts access. Liberty Ascent II, $1,200–$1,500, 4–6 hours, quietest and most serviceable. Gravity toilet plus ejector pit, $2,500–$6,000 professionally installed, 2–4 days, permanent. Every macerating system, regardless of brand, requires a dedicated GFCI outlet and a strict no-wipes, no-feminine-products rule — that discipline is the single biggest predictor of whether you'll love or hate an upflush toilet in year three.

Our Verdict

There's no universal winner here, so pick by scenario. If your basement is already finished and you're adding a bathroom without destroying the floor, Simple Project is the best value in 2026 — you get the same core functionality as Saniflo for hundreds less, plus smart features nobody else offers at that price, and it's the right call for a guest bath, a home-gym powder room, or a secondary bathroom that sees moderate use. If this will be a daily-use family or tenant bathroom, spend the extra on the Liberty Pumps Ascent II; the removable pump cartridge and quieter motor genuinely pay for themselves over a decade. If you need parts on a Saturday afternoon from a local supplier, Saniflo's distribution is unbeatable and worth its premium. And if your slab is already exposed during a full basement renovation, or you're creating a legal rental suite, install the traditional gravity toilet with a sewage ejector pit — it costs several times more upfront but it's silent, jam-proof, adds the most appraised value, and you'll never think about it again. Match the tool to the job, not the marketing.

Frequently Asked Questions

How loud is a macerating toilet compared to a regular toilet?

A macerator runs roughly 55–60 dB for a 10–15 second cycle after each flush — comparable to a kitchen garbage disposal or a loud dishwasher. Liberty Pumps' Ascent II is the quietest of the units compared here; Saniflo units are generally the loudest. A traditional gravity toilet has no motor at all and is effectively silent, which matters if the bathroom shares a wall with a bedroom or media room.

Do macerating toilets like Simple Project or Saniflo pass building code for a basement bathroom?

In most US jurisdictions, yes — macerating systems are recognized under IPC and UPC provisions for sewage pumps, provided the unit is UL-listed, on a GFCI circuit, and properly vented. However, some local inspectors and rental-licensing authorities require a gravity-drained fixture as the sole toilet in a legal dwelling unit. Always confirm with your local building department before buying, especially for a basement apartment.

What actually breaks on a macerating toilet, and how long do they last?

Expect 7–12 years with reasonable care. The most common failures are blade jams from flushed wipes, tampons or dental floss, and eventual motor or pressure-switch wear. Nothing but human waste and toilet paper should go in. Serviceability is the real brand differentiator: Liberty's cartridge lifts out in minutes, Saniflo's access panels open from the front, and Simple Project units generally require pulling the housing — cheaper to buy, more work to service.
