Framed vs. Unframed Large Wall Art: Which Should You Buy for Your Living Room? Big Wall Decor

⏱10 min read
By Best Home Decor Picks Daily • September 02, 2026 • Contains affiliate links

The framed-versus-unframed debate sounds like a style question, but in practice it's mostly a budget, shipping and wall-height question. A 60 x 40 inch gallery-wrapped canvas can hang the day it arrives and weighs a few pounds. The same image in a solid wood float frame with museum glass can cost two to three times more, weigh 25+ pounds, and require anchors into studs. Both look great above a sofa. Only one of them is going to make your delivery driver curse.

To make this a real decision rather than a vibe, we compared four places people actually buy oversized living room art: Big Wall Decor, which specialises in genuinely large statement canvases; iCanvas, the high-volume US canvas print house; Desenio, the Scandinavian poster brand that sells unframed prints plus cheap frames; and Great Big Canvas, which has one of the widest size ranges in the industry and offers both framed and unframed finishes.

Below is where each one actually wins — because the honest answer is that the best choice depends on your ceiling height, your wall colour, and whether you're decorating a rental or a forever home.

Big Wall Decor — best for true oversized statement canvases

Big Wall Decor is built around one idea: most people buy art that is too small for their wall. Their catalogue leans heavily into abstract, textured neutrals, minimalist line work and moody landscapes at sizes that actually fill the space above a three-seat sofa — the kind of pieces that read as one confident statement instead of a timid 24 x 36. Most of the range ships as gallery-wrapped canvas with the image continuing around a 1.25–1.5 inch stretcher bar, which means no frame required and no glass to fight glare from your living room window. Practically, that matters: an unframed 48 x 72 canvas is light enough for two drywall anchors and a French cleat, and it arrives ready to hang. Where Big Wall Decor is weaker is breadth — you won't find hundreds of thousands of licensed images or a huge catalogue of ornate traditional frames. It's a curated, contemporary look, and if that's your aesthetic, the scale-for-price ratio is the main reason to shop there.

iCanvas — best for licensed imagery and museum reproductions

iCanvas has an enormous licensed library: Van Gogh, Klimt, NASA imagery, Banksy-style street art, plus thousands of independent artists. They print on cotton-poly canvas, offer 0.75 inch and 1.5 inch depths, and sell framed float-frame options in black, white and walnut. Their sizing goes large but multi-panel sets are how they get you to true oversized coverage, which means you're aligning three or five panels on the wall yourself — doable, but it's a two-person job with a laser level. Pricing on a single large canvas is often comparable to Big Wall Decor before discounts, but iCanvas runs aggressive site-wide sales, so timing matters. Choose iCanvas if you want a specific famous image or a very particular subject; choose a specialist if you want one giant abstract piece.

Desenio — cheapest entry point, but you're buying paper

Desenio built its business on affordable unframed posters in Scandinavian and gallery-wall styles, with slim aluminium and wood frames sold separately. For a rental or a first apartment, it's hard to beat: a large poster can cost less than a takeaway, and their frames are inexpensive. The catch is scale and material. Desenio's largest common sizes top out around 70 x 100 cm (roughly 28 x 40 inches), which is a gallery-wall piece, not a sofa-spanning statement. Paper prints also need glazing to survive humidity and hands, and their acrylic glazing shows glare and can bow slightly in bigger frames. Desenio wins on price-per-print and on flexibility for a multi-frame arrangement; it loses badly if you want one dominant piece over a 90-inch sofa.

Great Big Canvas — best for extreme sizes and framed options

Great Big Canvas offers some of the largest stock sizes online, with canvases running well past 60 inches wide and framed options including float frames and traditional mouldings. If you specifically want framed art — a float frame around a canvas, or a matted print under glass — they have far more configuration choices than the canvas-first specialists. That flexibility comes with cost and freight: framed pieces at large sizes get heavy and expensive to ship, and returns on oversized framed goods are rarely painless anywhere. Their image library is stock-photography heavy, which is fine for landscapes and coastal scenes but can feel less curated than a design-led catalogue.

So which finish should you actually choose?

Unframed gallery-wrapped canvas suits modern and transitional living rooms, high-contrast walls, and anyone hanging above 60 inches wide — it's lighter, cheaper per square inch, and has no glare. Framed art suits paper prints, photography, traditional or panelled rooms, and anywhere you want a crisp visual border to separate the artwork from a busy wall colour or wallpaper. If your sofa is 84 inches or more, aim for artwork roughly two-thirds that width; at that scale, framing costs escalate fast and unframed canvas usually becomes the sensible choice.

Pros & Cons

Pros
  • ✓ Genuinely oversized canvases as single pieces, rather than relying on multi-panel splits like iCanvas to fill a large wall
  • ✓ Gallery-wrapped, ready-to-hang construction means no framing cost, no glass glare and far lighter hanging hardware than framed options from Great Big Canvas
  • ✓ Curated contemporary abstract and minimalist range — easier to shop than iCanvas's sprawling licensed library if you just want something that works over a sofa
  • ✓ Far better scale for money than Desenio, whose largest common size (around 28 x 40 inches) is too small to anchor a living room wall
Cons
  • ✗ Much smaller catalogue than iCanvas or Great Big Canvas — no famous museum reproductions or deep stock photography archive
  • ✗ Canvas-first approach means fewer traditional framed and matted-under-glass configurations than Great Big Canvas offers

Our Verdict

There's no universal winner here. If you want one large, contemporary, ready-to-hang statement piece above a sofa and you don't want to deal with framing costs or heavy freight, Big Wall Decor is the most direct route to that outcome. If you're after a specific licensed image — a Klimt, a NASA shot, a named artist — iCanvas has the library and frequent sales to beat it on price. If you specifically want framed art with float frames, mats or traditional mouldings, Great Big Canvas gives you the widest configuration choice, provided you accept the shipping weight and cost. And if you're renting, decorating on a tight budget, or building a gallery wall of six to nine smaller pieces, Desenio is still the cheapest sensible option — just don't expect it to fill a big blank wall on its own.

Frequently Asked Questions

How big should living room wall art be above a sofa?

Aim for artwork that spans roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of the sofa width, with the centre of the piece around 57–60 inches from the floor. For an 84-inch sofa that means something in the 56–63 inch range — a size where unframed canvas is usually far more practical and affordable than framed.

Is unframed canvas art considered cheap-looking?

Not if the wrap is done properly. A gallery wrap with a 1.25–1.5 inch stretcher bar and the image continuing around the sides reads as intentional and modern. Thin 0.75 inch wraps with white or mirrored edges are the ones that look budget. Paper posters tacked up unframed are a different thing entirely — those genuinely do need a frame.

Can I add a frame to a canvas later?

Yes — a float frame is designed exactly for this and clips around a stretched canvas without glass. It typically adds an inch or so to each dimension, so measure your wall with that allowance in mind. Framing shops and several online retailers sell float frames in standard canvas sizes, which is a good way to upgrade an unframed piece later without rebuying the art.
