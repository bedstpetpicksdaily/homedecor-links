What Wattage Bulb Is Best for a Bedside Table Lamp?

For a bedside table lamp, the best bulb is one that produces 400 to 800 lumens, which means a 40-watt to 60-watt incandescent bulb or a 5-watt to 9-watt LED equivalent. If you read in bed, aim for the higher end (60W equivalent / 800 lumens); if the lamp is purely for ambient light and winding down, 40W equivalent (450 lumens) is plenty.

The Short Answer

Stick with a 40W to 60W equivalent bulb for a bedside table lamp — that's a 5W to 9W LED putting out roughly 450 to 800 lumens. Choose a warm color temperature between 2200K and 2700K so the light doesn't interfere with your body's melatonin production before sleep. Never exceed the wattage rating printed on your lamp's socket or harp, which is typically 60W maximum for most table lamps. If you want a single bulb that works for both reading and relaxing, a dimmable 60W-equivalent LED is the smartest choice.

The Full Explanation

Wattage is a measurement of energy consumption, not brightness — a distinction that matters enormously now that LEDs have replaced incandescent bulbs in most homes. An old 60-watt incandescent bulb and a modern 9-watt LED can produce identical light output, but the LED uses 85% less electricity. This is why lighting professionals now talk in lumens rather than watts.

Here's how the two scales line up for bedside lighting:

  • 25W incandescent = 2-3W LED = ~200 lumens. Too dim for anything but a nightlight glow. Suitable only if you have multiple light sources in the room.
  • 40W incandescent = 4-5W LED = ~450 lumens. The sweet spot for ambient bedside light. Enough to see by, soft enough to feel restful. Ideal for small bedrooms or lamps placed close to your head.
  • 60W incandescent = 8-9W LED = ~800 lumens. The most popular bedside wattage. Bright enough for comfortable reading without glare, and still cozy under a fabric shade.
  • 75W+ incandescent = 11W+ LED = 1,100+ lumens. Too harsh for most bedside applications and often exceeds the lamp's safety rating. Reserve this for floor lamps and overhead fixtures.

Your lampshade changes everything. A dark, opaque drum shade can absorb 50% or more of a bulb's output, meaning a 450-lumen bulb might deliver the practical brightness of 225 lumens. A white or cream linen shade diffuses light beautifully while letting most of it through. If your bedside lamp has a dark shade — black, navy, charcoal, or a heavy textured fabric — bump up one step to a 60W equivalent. If it has a light, translucent shade, 40W equivalent will feel just right.

Color temperature matters as much as wattage. Measured in Kelvin, this determines whether light appears warm and yellow or cool and blue. For bedrooms, stay between 2200K and 2700K. Anything above 3000K starts pushing into the blue-white spectrum that suppresses melatonin and signals your brain to stay alert — exactly what you don't want at 10 p.m. Some LED bulbs now offer "warm dim" technology that shifts toward candlelight amber as you lower the brightness, mimicking a sunset.

Check your lamp's maximum wattage rating. Every UL-listed lamp has a label near the socket specifying the maximum incandescent wattage it can safely handle — usually 40W or 60W for table lamps. Exceeding it risks scorching the socket, damaging wiring, and creating a genuine fire hazard. The good news: because LEDs run dramatically cooler and consume less power, a 9W LED producing 800 lumens is perfectly safe in a lamp rated for 60W maximum, even though it delivers "60W-equivalent" brightness.

Consider bulb shape and visibility. If your lamp has an open-top shade or a clear glass body, the bulb itself will be visible from certain angles. A frosted A19 bulb or a decorative filament-style ST19 will look far better than a bare, glaring LED with visible diodes. For lamps where the bulb is fully concealed, shape is irrelevant — prioritize output and dimmability instead.

What the Experts Say

Interior lighting designers consistently recommend building bedroom illumination in layers rather than relying on a single bright source. Bedside lamps fall into the "task and ambient" category — they need to serve double duty as reading light and evening mood light, which is precisely why designers push clients toward dimmable fixtures rather than obsessing over a single perfect wattage.

The American Lighting Association recommends approximately 400 to 500 lumens of task lighting for reading in bed, positioned so the bottom of the lampshade sits roughly at shoulder height when you're seated upright against the headboard — typically 20 to 24 inches above the mattress. Position matters as much as brightness: a lamp that's too tall will shine the bulb directly into your eyes, while one that's too short will cast light on your lap instead of your book.

Sleep researchers add an important caveat that lighting catalogs rarely mention. Studies on circadian rhythm consistently show that light exposure above roughly 100 lux in the hours before bed delays melatonin onset and reduces sleep quality. Practically speaking, this means the ability to dim your bedside lamp is more valuable than the maximum brightness it can achieve. The ideal setup is a lamp that can deliver 800 lumens when you're reading at 9 p.m. and drop to 50 lumens when you're setting your alarm at 11.

Electricians and lighting retailers also flag a common frustration: not all LED bulbs dim smoothly. Standard LEDs paired with older triac dimmer switches often flicker, buzz, or cut out entirely below 20% brightness. Look specifically for bulbs labeled "dimmable" and, ideally, lamps with integrated dimming built into the base — which sidesteps compatibility problems altogether.

The Product Solution

The cleanest way to solve the wattage question is to stop guessing and buy a lamp that adjusts on demand. A dimmable LED bedside table lamp with USB ports eliminates the entire calculation — instead of committing to 40W or 60W, you get the full range at the touch of a control, from a bright reading setting down to a barely-there glow for navigating the room at 2 a.m.

These lamps solve three bedside problems at once. First, the integrated LED means no bulb shopping, no wattage-rating anxiety, and no replacement for 20,000-plus hours of use. Second, built-in touch or slider dimming avoids the flicker and buzz that plague standard dimmable bulbs on incompatible wall switches. Third — and this is the underrated feature — the USB ports mean your phone charges at the nightstand without an ugly wall-wart adapter and a cable snaking across the floor. Many models include both USB-A and USB-C, plus a standard AC outlet for a white noise machine or an alarm clock.

From a decor standpoint, these lamps have improved dramatically. You're no longer limited to clinical-looking plastic units. Current options include fabric drum shades, ceramic and ribbed glass bases, brushed brass and matte black finishes, and slim column designs that suit small nightstands. Look for models offering multiple color temperatures — the best include a 2700K warm setting for evening and a brighter 4000K option for mornings — and check that the touch controls are on the base rather than requiring you to reach up under a hot shade.

Quick Tips

  • Match wattage to your shade: Dark or opaque shades absorb light, so choose a 60W-equivalent bulb (800 lumens). Light or translucent shades work well with a 40W-equivalent (450 lumens).
  • Never exceed the socket rating: Check the label near the socket for the maximum incandescent wattage. LEDs stay safe because a 9W LED draws far less power than the 60W rating allows.
  • Stay under 2700K: Warm color temperatures between 2200K and 2700K protect melatonin production. Save cool white and daylight bulbs for kitchens, closets, and bathrooms.
  • Prioritize dimming over brightness: A lamp that adjusts from 800 lumens down to near-candlelight beats any fixed-wattage bulb for bedroom use, and it eliminates the need to choose at all.

Conclusion

For most bedrooms, a 60-watt-equivalent LED bulb — roughly 800 lumens at 2700K — is the safest all-around choice for a bedside table lamp, with 40W-equivalent working better for pure ambient light or lamps positioned very close to your pillow. The smartest move, though, is to skip the guesswork entirely and choose a dimmable lamp that lets you dial in exactly the right brightness for reading, relaxing, and everything in between.
