Best Wall Art for Bedrooms

Your bedroom is the one room in your home that greets you first thing in the morning and sends you off at night — which means the art on those walls does more emotional work than anything hanging in your living room or hallway. Get it right, and the space feels like exhaling. Get it wrong, and you've got a piece that quietly agitates you every time you glance up from your pillow, even if you can't put your finger on why.

The good news: bedroom wall art follows a different, gentler set of rules than the rest of the house, and once you understand them, choosing becomes much easier.

Choose wall art with a color palette that complements your bedding and existing furniture to create visual cohesion, rather than selecting pieces solely based on personal preference. Consider the scale of your artwork relative to your wall space and bed size—oversized pieces work best on larger walls, while smaller pieces should be grouped together to avoid looking lost in the room.

Editor's Tip

What to Look For

  • A low-contrast, restful palette. Bedrooms are for winding down, so look for art with soft value transitions — muted blues, warm neutrals, dusty terracotta, sage, washed-out sepia. High-contrast black-and-white graphics and saturated primaries read as stimulating, which is exactly what you don't want at 10 p.m. If you love bold color, save it for a small accent piece rather than the headboard wall.
  • Scale that matches your bed, not your wall. The most common bedroom art mistake is hanging something proportioned to the room instead of to the furniture below it. Art above a headboard should span roughly two-thirds the width of the bed — about 40–48" for a queen, 48–60" for a king. Anything narrower floats awkwardly; anything wider crowds the nightstands and lamps.
  • Non-glare, lightweight construction. Bedside lamps throw light at low, raking angles that turn glass into a mirror. Canvas, textile, or matte-finish prints solve this instantly. Lightweight also matters for safety — nobody wants a heavy framed piece over their head, especially in earthquake-prone areas or on plaster walls.

Our Top Pick

A large-format abstract landscape on gallery-wrapped canvas, in a soft horizon palette (warm cream, misty blue-grey, and a faint blush band), sized 48" x 32" for queens or 60" x 40" for kings.

This is the piece we recommend most often for bedrooms, and for very practical reasons. The horizontal orientation mirrors the shape of the bed and reinforces the room's natural sense of rest — vertical art pulls the eye up and energizes a space, which is great for entryways and terrible above a headboard. The abstract horizon format gives your eye somewhere to settle without demanding interpretation; it's calming in the way a window view is calming, without the literalism of a photograph you'll eventually get tired of.

The gallery-wrapped canvas construction handles the two bedroom-specific problems at once: no glass means no lamp glare bouncing back at you while you read, and a stretched canvas at that size typically weighs under eight pounds — safe on a single well-anchored hook or a French cleat. The soft palette also plays nicely with bedding, which is the busiest textile surface in the room. A muted piece lets you swap duvet covers seasonally without the art suddenly clashing.

Why This Works for This Situation

Bedrooms are viewed from an unusual angle — mostly lying down, mostly in low light, mostly by two people with different tastes. A soft abstract landscape works because it doesn't insist on a single reading. There's no subject to disagree about, no message to get tired of, and no hard edges that catch the eye when you're trying to fall asleep. It functions almost architecturally, adding depth and a sense of expanded space to what is often the smallest room on the floor plan.

There's also a coordination advantage that's easy to overlook. The bedroom is a layered room by nature — rug, curtains, throw pillows, lamp shades, a bench at the foot of the bed. A large piece of quiet art acts as the connective tissue between all of it. Pull the blush tone into your throw pillows, echo the misty blue in your curtain panels, and let the cream tie back to the rug. You get a room that reads as intentional and collected rather than a set of separately purchased objects. A busier, more dominant piece of art would force everything else to defer to it, which limits your options every time you want to refresh the space.

What to Avoid

  • Framed art under glass above the bed. Beyond the glare issue, the safety concern is real and worth taking seriously. If you already own a framed piece you love, hang it on the wall opposite the bed or above a dresser instead, and use a canvas or textile piece over the headboard.
  • Tight gallery walls above the headboard. Gallery walls are visually busy by design — lots of edges, lots of small decisions for your eye to process. They're wonderful in a stairwell or dining room and restless in a bedroom. If you want multiple pieces, use a simple diptych or triptych in matching frames with generous, even spacing.
  • Motivational typography and literal wall quotes. "Live Laugh Love," "Sweet Dreams," and their cousins date fast and give the room a showroom quality. Text also engages the reading part of your brain, which is precisely the part you're trying to switch off. If you want personality, choose something with texture or hand-made character instead — a woven wall hanging, a botanical study, an abstract with visible brushwork.
  • Anything hung too high. Bedroom art should sit about 6–10 inches above the headboard, not floating at standard gallery height. Too much gap and the piece looks disconnected from the bed entirely.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size wall art should I hang above my bed?

For a standard queen or full bed, choose artwork that's 50-60% the width of your bed frame, typically 36-48 inches wide. Above a king bed, opt for 48-60 inches wide to maintain proper visual proportion without overwhelming the space.

What height should I hang bedroom wall art?

Center your artwork 60 inches from the floor to the middle of the piece, or hang it 6-12 inches above your headboard if mounted directly above the bed. This creates an eye-level focal point whether you're standing or lying down.

What type of wall art works best in small bedrooms?

Vertical or tall narrow pieces work better than horizontal spreads in small bedrooms as they draw the eye upward and make rooms feel larger. A single statement piece or gallery arrangement on one accent wall is more effective than scattered art throughout the room.

Should bedroom wall art match my bedding or curtains?

Your wall art should complement your bedding by sharing a color palette or theme, but doesn't need to match exactly—aim for 2-3 coordinating colors rather than identical patterns. This creates visual cohesion while allowing the art to stand as a distinct focal point rather than blending into the background.
